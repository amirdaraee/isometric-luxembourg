## Era 1 – Roman roads and the Bock before 963

**Facts.**
- Two Roman roads crossed on the plateau above the Alzette and Pétrusse: **Arlon (Orolaunum) – Trier (Augusta Treverorum)** and a road south to **Thionville / Metz**. The crossing lay roughly where the **Place du Marché-aux-Poissons** is now; the city says a fortified tower stood there in late Roman times.
- On the **Bock** promontory stood a small late-Roman fortification (castellum); its name, **Lucilinburhuc** ("little castle"), was carried on by Siegfried's castle and became Lützelburg → Luxembourg.
- Wider area: the Roman vicus **Ricciacum** at Dalheim on the Metz–Trier road (theatre, baths); Roman villas around the Alzette; finds in the MNHA.
- After the Romans the fort decayed; the land belonged to **St Maximin's Abbey in Trier** until 963.

**What it looked like.** A wooded/agricultural plateau cut by two gorges, the Bock a narrow rock spur above the Alzette loop, a road crossing with a tower or palisade, nothing like a town.

**What survives.** Nothing visible in the old town. The street names (Marché-aux-Poissons, rue de la Loge) follow the old crossing; Roman finds are in the MNHA (Marché-aux-Poissons). Dalheim's Ricciacum theatre is the nearest visible Roman site.

**Images.** `1-roman__ricciacum-dalheim__roman-theatre.jpg` only. There is no freely licensed view or plan of Roman Luxembourg; a snapshot would have to be invented from the terrain (task 3 `fortress__ferraris-map-c1775.jpg` and the modern DEM give the terrain).

## Era 2 – 963: Count Siegfried's castle (Lucilinburhuc)

**Facts.**
- **12 April 963**: Count **Siegfried** (of the House of Ardennes) acquired the Bock and the ruined fort from St Maximin's Abbey, Trier, in exchange for lands at Feulen near Ettelbruck. This is the city's founding date.
- He built a **castle on the Bock**, cut off from the plateau by a rock ditch and reached by a **drawbridge** (today's **Schlassbréck / Pont du Château**, rebuilt in stone in 1735).
- Knights and soldiers lived on the rock; traders and craftsmen settled on the plateau beside it around the market (today's Fishmarket) – the start of the upper town / lower town split.
- **St Michael's Church** (Saint-Michel) began as the castle chapel (987); the present church is 17th c. on the old site.
- The castle grew over the 11th–14th c. into a group of buildings on the Bock: keep, chapel, halls, towers. From the 15th c. it lost its residential role; it was badly damaged in the sieges (1684 French bombardment), later reduced to military use, and what was left was razed during the dismantling after 1867. Only foundations remain.

**What it looked like.** A fortified complex strung along the narrow Bock spur (about 300 m long), with steep rock on three sides, a ditch and drawbridge on the town side; the hamlet below and on the plateau to the west. Best visual: Ernest Werling's 1890 reconstruction drawing.

**What survives.** Foundations and wall stumps of the castle on top of the Bock (visible from the Montée de Clausen and the Bock viewpoint); the **Schlassbréck** bridge; the **Bock casemates** dug into the same rock (1644 onwards, see task 3); Saint-Michel church on the castle chapel site; the "Mélusine" legend tied to the rock.

**Images and maps.**

## Era 3 – The medieval walls (c. 1050 – 1443)

**Facts.** Sources differ in numbering and dates; the sequence below follows the City of Luxembourg's own summary and the LCTO Wenzel walk.

| Ring | Date | Course (today's streets) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| First wall | c. 1050 (some sources: late 11th–12th c.) | Small enclosure around the castle approach and the market at the **Marché-aux-Poissons**, between the Bock and the head of the Montée du Grund | Earliest town wall; Dräi Tierm-type towers start in this period |
| Second wall | 12th c. | Ran along today's **rue du Fossé** ("ditch street"), parallel to the first; took in the market area up to the Krautmaart (Marché-aux-Herbes) | The fossé (ditch) gave the street its name |
| Third wall (John the Blind) | from **1320**, completed **1398** | Enclosed the whole upper town to the line of today's **Boulevard Royal / Boulevard Roosevelt** (west) and took in **Grund, Pfaffenthal, the Rham plateau and the Neumünster abbey** | Built by John the Blind and finished under Wenceslas II |
| **Wenzel wall** (Wenzelsmauer) | **1390s**, under Duke **Wenceslas II** (r. 1383–1419) | From the Bock down across the Alzette at the Grund to the **Rham plateau**, closing off the lower town | Originally **875 m** with **37 towers and 15 gates** (LCTO) |

- In the Middle Ages the town was relatively open: some **23 gates** let people and goods through. From the 16th c. the new bastions buried them: the **Porte Marie** under Bastion Marie (1548), the **Lampert, Orvis, Beckerich and Jost gates** under the Berlaimont, Louis, Beck and Jost bastions (early 17th c.).
- **Porte des Juifs (Jews' Gate)**: the main western gate at the end of the Grand-Rue / rue des Juifs (later rue de l'Arsenal, today around the west end of Grand-Rue / Boulevard Royal), the main trade route to the Low Countries. **Closed in 1644**; traffic was diverted to the **Porte-Neuve** (built 1626–1636), which then forced travellers from France down through the Grund and up via the Fishmarket.
- **Trierer Tor (Porte de Trèves)**: the eastern gates where the road to Trier left the town across the Alzette (Grund/Clausen side). The "three Trier gates" sequence survives in part near the Bock and the Wenzel wall (1st Trier gate = "Dinselpaart").
- **Dräi Tierm (Trois Tours, Porte de Pfaffenthal)**: three towers on the north side above Pfaffenthal; the oldest part dates to about 1050, the other two 14th c.; used as a gate until 1867 and spared from demolition.
- **Tour Jacob** (Jacobsturm), **Tour Orth**, the **Grund gate** and **Malakoff tower** belong to the Wenzel / Rham defences (Malakoff tower is 19th c.).
- **Spanish turrets (Spuenesch Tiermercher)**: small round watch-turrets (17th c., Spanish period) on the walls, e.g. above the Pétrusse and at the Wenzel wall – often placed on the medieval line.
- **1354**: Luxembourg raised from county to **duchy** by Charles IV.

**What it looked like.** A compact walled town on the plateau, its western wall roughly on Boulevard Royal, the castle on the Bock, the churches of Saint-Michel and Saint-Nicolas (site of today's Chamber/palace area), the Franciscan convent (Knodler, today Place Guillaume II), the market squares (Fishmarket, Krautmaart), and a separate walled lower town in the Grund with the Neumünster abbey (then on the Altmünster site above the town until 1543). Deventer's plan of c. 1550–1565 is the best image of this compact medieval town just before the bastions arrived.

**What survives.** **Wenzel wall** (Bock to Rham, with the Grund gate and towers, walkable "Wenzel walk"); **Dräi Tierm**; parts of the Trier gates; **Tour Jacob**; wall stretches on the Rham; the street pattern of the Ville Haute (Grand-Rue, rue du Fossé, Marché-aux-Herbes). OSM features: see `data/osm_walls_gates_towers_additions.geojson` and task 3's file (`historic=citywalls`, way 831169742 "Mur de Wenceslas").

## Era 4 – The Burgundian, Spanish, French and Austrian fortress (1443 – 1795)

**Facts.**
- **1443 siege**: on the night of 21–22 November 1443 Burgundian troops of **Philip the Good** took the town by surprise, scaling the walls at night; the castle garrison held out a little longer. Start of four centuries of foreign rule. 1477: passes to the Habsburgs by marriage.
- **1542–44**: briefly taken by Francis I of France, then retaken.
- **c. 1545 onwards** (Charles V): Italian and Dutch engineers build the **first bastions and curtains** on the west front (today's Boulevard Roosevelt / Boulevard Royal), widen the ditch from 13 to 31 m, add ravelins.
- **Spanish period** (1556–1684, and 1698–1701): 1644 onwards **Isaac van Treybach** starts the **casemates** in the Bock and under the Beck bastion; **1672** Louvigny builds the advanced redoubts (Peter, Louvigny, Marie, Berlaimont) and the first barracks.
- **Siege of 1684**: blockade from late 1683, French bombardment Dec 1683; the formal siege under **Marshal de Créquy** with **Vauban** directing the attack ran from **28 April to 3 June 1684** (trenches opened 8 May). Painted by **Van der Meulen** and Pierre-Denis Martin.
- **Vauban's works 1684–1688** (3,000+ workers): crownwork **Niedergrünewald**, hornwork **Obergrünewald**, **Fort Bourbon**, the Verlorenkost "corniche", redoubts; **Pfaffenthal** brought inside the defences with the **Eich gate** and **Bons-Malades gate**; large barracks on the **Rham** and **Saint-Esprit** plateaux (Saint-Esprit citadel, 1685).
- 1698 back to Spain (Ryswick), 1701 French again, 1713/15 Dutch then **Austrian** (1715–1795).
- **Austrian additions** (engineer **Simon de Bauffe** and others, mostly 1730s–1790s): forts **Olizy, Thüngen (1732), Rubamprez, Rumigny, Neipperg, Wallis, Rheinsheim, Charles**, lunettes, sluice-dams closing the valleys (Grund lock, Mansfeld lock), and the bulk of the **c. 23 km of casemates** (40,000 m² of bomb-proof space). 1749: no-build security perimeter; Neipperg strips the glacis to bare rock 600 m out ("champs pelés"). Triple line of defence on every side.
- **"Gibraltar of the North"**: Carnot's report to the Convention in 1795 called it "the best fortress in Europe after Gibraltar".
- **Siege / blockade of 1794–95**: French Revolutionary army blockaded from **November 1794**; the Austrian garrison under **Field Marshal Bender** surrendered on **7 June 1795** after about seven months, walls largely unbreached, through hunger and disease. The fortress went to France (Département des Forêts, 1795–1814). Goethe sketched the fortress in October 1792.
- Final layout: 3 rings, c. 180 ha of fortifications around a c. 120 ha town, 15 inner and 9 outer forts.

**What it looked like.** The medieval town (still the same street plan) wrapped in bastioned fronts: west (plain front: Bastions Marie, Berlaimont, Louis, Beck, Jost), north-east (Grünewald front with Thüngen, Olizy, Ober- and Niedergrünewald), east (Trier front over the Rham), south (Thionville front across the Pétrusse: Fort Bourbon, Wallis, Elisabeth, Neipperg). Pfaffenthal and Grund in the valleys closed by walls and sluices. Bare glacis all round. The **Mansfeld palace "La Fontaine"** in Clausen (1563–1604, Count Peter Ernst von Mansfeld, Spanish governor) with its famous gardens stood below until it decayed in the 17th–18th c.

**What survives.** Fort Thüngen (Dräi Eechelen, rebuilt 2012 as MUDAM/M3E – task 1), Fort Obergrünewald remains, Saint-Esprit citadel, Bock and Pétrusse casemates, Beck bastion (Place de la Constitution), Vauban towers in Pfaffenthal, Eich and Bons-Malades gates, Rham barracks, Grund lock, Mansfeld garden gate and ruins in Clausen (`Schlass La Fontaine`, OSM way 164179103). Full surviving-works footprint: `../03-underground-tunnels/data/unesco_existing_fortress.geojson`.

## Era 5 – Federal fortress of the German Confederation, Prussian garrison (1815 – 1867)

**Facts.**
- Prussian troops in the fortress from **8 July 1814**; Congress of Vienna (**9 June 1815**) makes Luxembourg a Grand Duchy in personal union with the Netherlands and the fortress a **federal fortress** of the German Confederation; Prussia appoints the governor. Garrison officially ¾ Prussian, ¼ Dutch, in practice all Prussian (exclusive right from 1856), about **4,000 men** among **10,000–13,000** civilians.
- 1830–39: during the Belgian Revolution the fortress stayed loyal to William I while the rest of the country was run from Brussels; 1839 Treaty of London fixes today's borders.
- Prussian works: modernisation, **Fort Wedell** and **Fort Dumoulin**, plans for a fourth outer ring never built. No-build perimeter extended to **979 m**.
- **Railway**: first station (1859) on the Bourbon plateau, built in **wood** because it lay in the perimeter; the **Passerelle / Viaduc** (1859–61) and the Pulvermühl viaducts.
- 1822 **original cadastre**: the first parcel-level survey of the town (sheets downloaded here, see below) – every house footprint inside the walls.
- Turner visited in 1825 and 1839 and left dozens of sketches and watercolours; Michel Engels later (1887) drew the fortress "as it was" around 1860–67.

**What it looked like.** Fortress at its maximum extent: three rings of bastions, forts on every height (Thüngen, Olizy, Bourbon, Wallis, Charles, Louvigny, Berlaimont, Wedell, Dumoulin), dense town of narrow tall houses inside, Grund/Clausen/Pfaffenthal in the valleys, gates closed at night, bare glacis, the railway viaduct crossing the Pétrusse at the edge. The 1867 Biermann plan (task 3) shows exactly this state.

**What survives.** Most of what survives of the fortress at all; the Passerelle viaduct; Prussian Fort Dumoulin remains; the Vauban and Wenzel walks.

## Era 6 – Treaty of London and the dismantling (1867 – 1883)

**Facts.**
- **Luxembourg Crisis** 1866–67 (Napoleon III's offer to buy Luxembourg from William III). **Second Treaty of London, 11 May 1867**: Luxembourg perpetually neutral, fortress to be demolished, Prussian garrison out within three months. The last Prussians left on **9 September 1867**.
- Demolition by the state, **1867–1883**, about 1.5 million francs (city figure: 1.8 million gold francs), financed by selling the fortress land. Works were blown up, stone carted off, the rest buried; casemates largely walled up (only ~17 km of the 23 km survive). An international commission inspected in 1883.
- **What was razed**: the whole western plain front (bastions Marie, Berlaimont, Louis, Beck partly, Jost), the Porte-Neuve (1871–75), the Porte des Juifs line, most gates, the castle remains on the Bock, most forts (Louvigny, Berlaimont, Marie, Royal, Vauban, Lambert, Charles, Bourbon, Elisabeth, Wallis, Neipperg, Wedell, Olizy and the Grünewald works buried), barracks, glacis.
- **What survived** (kept on purpose as landmarks): the **Dräi Tierm**, the **Vauban towers** in Pfaffenthal, the gate of **Fort Thüngen** (three "acorns"), **Tour Jacob** and the Rham barracks towers, the **Saint-Esprit citadel**, the **Schlassbréck**, some **Spanish turrets**, the Bock and Pétrusse casemates, the Wenzel wall, the Beck bastion terrace.
- **What replaced the walls**: **Boulevard Royal** and **Boulevard Grande-Duchesse Charlotte / Roosevelt** on the line of the western front; **Avenue Monterey** cut through in 1869; the **Municipal Park** (Stadtpark) laid out **1871–1878** on the plain-front glacis to a plan by French landscape architect **Édouard André**, with villas along it (Villa Vauban 1873, Villa Pauly etc.); Avenue de la Porte-Neuve, Avenue Émile Reuter; Place de Metz and the Bourbon plateau opened for building; the new residential quarters of **Limpertsberg** and **Belair** began.

**What it looked like.** A city in transition: open breaches in the bastions, rubble slopes, new boulevards on the old ditches, a young park with curving paths where the glacis was, the station quarter starting on the Bourbon plateau across the viaduct.

## Era 7 – Growth of the open city (1883 – today)

**Facts and milestones.**
- **Gare quarter**: the stone station replaces the wooden one (present building 1907–1913); Avenue de la Gare and Avenue de la Liberté (laid out after 1903), Place de Paris; State Savings Bank (BCEE, 1913), ARBED headquarters (1922). Hollerich and Bonnevoie grow as industrial / railway suburbs.
- **Pont Adolphe** (1900–1903, engineer Paul Séjourné, 84.65 m span, at the time the largest stone arch in the world) links the old town to the Bourbon plateau and opens the Gare quarter. Bernhoeft photographed the works.
- **Limpertsberg** and **Belair** develop as residential quarters on the former glacis (1870s–1930s); the Glacis remains as an open space (Schueberfouer fairground).
- **1920 merger** (law of 26 March 1920): the communes of **Hollerich** (the south and west: Hollerich, Bonnevoie, Gasperich, Cessange, Merl), **Eich** (the north: Eich, Dommeldange, Beggen, Weimerskirch), **Hamm** (east) and **Rollingergrund** (north-west) join the city (exact village lists per commune: see the polygons). The municipality grows from about 3 km² to 51.5 km² and to about 46,000 people. Boundaries: `data/communes_before_after_1920.geojson`.
- **WWII**: German invasion 10 May 1940; civil administration under Gauleiter Gustav Simon, de facto annexation to the Reich (1942), Germanisation, the 1942 general strike; the Villa Pauly as Gestapo HQ; liberation of the city by US troops on **10 September 1944**; Grand Duchess Charlotte returned 14 April 1945. Little bomb damage in the city itself.
- **European capital**: ECSC High Authority in Luxembourg from 1952 (Place de Metz building, now BCEE); **Kirchberg plateau** developed from 1961 by the Fonds Kirchberg; **Pont Grande-Duchesse Charlotte (Red Bridge)** opened **1966**; European Parliament secretariat (Tour Alcide de Gasperi, 1966), Court of Justice (1973, towers 2008), EIB, European Commission services; Philharmonie (2005), MUDAM (2006) at Fort Thüngen.
- **Recent**: **tram** (Luxtram) opened 10 December 2017 on Kirchberg, then extended in stages through the centre to the Gare (2020) and on south towards Bonnevoie and the Cloche d'Or and east to Findel airport (2020s); **Pfaffenthal funicular and lift** (2016–17); **Cloche d'Or / Ban de Gasperich** new business and housing district (2010s–2020s, Cloche d'Or shopping centre 2019); population about 136,000 in 2025, ~70 % foreign nationals.

**What survives / what to show.** This is today's city; the snapshots are about which areas exist: c. 1900 (old town + Gare quarter + Limpertsberg/Belair, Pont Adolphe), c. 1950 (1920 municipality built up, no Kirchberg), 1970s (Kirchberg first towers, Red Bridge), today.

<a id="recommendation"></a>
## Recommendation: the most striking map snapshots

1. **c. 1050–1100 – "The count's castle"** (Era 2). Dramatic and simple: the Bock crowned by the castle with drawbridge, a small walled hamlet at the Fishmarket, empty plateau, wooded gorges. Draw from Werling's 1890 reconstruction (`2-castle__lucilinburhuc__werling-reconstruction-1890.jpg`), the Bock photos/paintings of the castle ruins (Engels 14 & 15, Turner's Bock sketches, the 1905 ruin postcard) and the modern terrain. Mostly invented detail, so keep it painterly.
2. **c. 1550 – "The walled medieval town"** (Era 3). Towers, gates, steep roofs, churches, the castle still standing, the Grund behind the Wenzel wall, Mansfeld's palace in Clausen. Sources: Deventer c. 1550 (layout), Braun & Hogenberg 1581/1616 and Guicciardini 1609 (towers, gates, roofscape), Engels' 16th-century reconstruction, Mansfeld palace views; survivals (Wenzel wall, Dräi Tierm) from task 1 photos.
3. **1684 – "The siege"** (Era 4) as an event snapshot: Van der Meulen's Louvre view, the 1684 attack plan and the blockade map, Martin's painting. Trenches, smoke, tents on the heights; the pre-Vauban fortress. Optional, but very striking.
4. **c. 1795–1867 – "The Gibraltar of the North"** (Eras 4–5), the strongest candidate and the best documented: full three-ring fortress, forts on every height, bare glacis, the dense town inside. Geometry from Lefort 1794 / the 1794 overlay and Biermann 1867 (task 3) and the 1822 cadastre (house footprints); 3D look from the 1805 plan-relief photos; colour and atmosphere from Turner (1825/1839), Callot 1753, Liez, and Engels' 25 plates; underground from task 3.
5. **c. 1905 – "The open city"** (Era 7): walls gone, Municipal Park and boulevards, Pont Adolphe brand-new, the Gare quarter going up, trams. Sources: Biermann 1907 and 1894 plans, Bernhoeft's 1899 panorama and Pont Adolphe photos, the Brück & Sohn postcards, André's park plan.

Today's map is the present snapshot; a 1920 boundary overlay (`communes_before_after_1920.geojson`) and a Kirchberg-before-1960 toggle are cheap extras.
